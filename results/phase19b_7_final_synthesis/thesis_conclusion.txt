1. THESIS ABSTRACT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Background:
Automated crop disease diagnosis using deep convolutional neural networks has demonstrated
exceptional classification accuracy on laboratory-curated benchmark datasets. However, real-world
agricultural deployment in developing regions such as Maharashtra, India, is plagued by severe
domain shift between controlled laboratory imagery and unconstrained field photography.

Methods:
We conducted a comprehensive, multi-phase investigation of lightweight transfer learning (MobileNetV2)
across 33 canonical disease classes spanning four economically vital crops: Tomato, Grape, Chilli,
and Sugarcane (31,590 images total). We evaluated production models across complete internal test splits
(Track A, n=4,741: Tomato 2,180, Grape 931, Chilli 290, Sugarcane 1,340) alongside an audited 4-track
empirical robustness framework (2,556 evaluations: Track A audit sample n=1,119, Track B independent external
research benchmarks n=632, Track C unconstrained real-world field photography n=425, and Track D out-of-distribution
stress tests n=380). To address identified vulnerabilities, we investigated post-hoc temperature scaling, a 4-class
species identifier, a binary leaf vs. non-leaf gatekeeper, and controlled field-data retraining using authentic in-situ field imagery.

Results:
While frozen MobileNetV2 achieved 83.4%–90.2% in-domain test accuracy, evaluation on uncontrolled
field benchmarks revealed a substantial Generalization Gap of 50 to 75 percentage points
(Tomato: 90.2% -> 18.9%; Grape: 89.0% -> 11.4%). Models collapsed into majority attractor sinks
(87.6% of Tomato field predictions defaulted to Early/Late Blight), while assigning >85%–95%
softmax confidence to non-leaf distractors. Post-hoc calibration reduced in-domain calibration error
by >50% but failed to reject OOD inputs (60%–73% false pass rate). A laboratory-trained species
identifier achieved 99.87% internal accuracy but collapsed to 39.7% in the field, where observed errors
were consistent with sensitivity to domain-specific visual characteristics. Conversely, a dedicated Stage 1
binary gatekeeper trained on curated non-leaf negatives showed strong retention across the evaluated
field-leaf benchmarks (96.55% field leaf retention across 2,229 uncurated field images) and 100% rejection
of Track D non-leaf distractors. In controlled retraining experiments, incorporating authentic field imagery
reduced the concentration of predictions in previously dominant attractor classes in some evaluated cases and
improved sensitivity to selected field phenotypes (Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus recall increased from 5.36% to
42.86%; Grape Downy Mildew recall increased from 4.00% to 26.00%), while causing modest reductions in internal
test performance (Tomato: 90.23% -> 89.72%, -0.50 pp; Grape: 89.04% -> 87.86%, -1.18 pp).

Conclusion:
Deep vision models trained on laboratory leaf datasets suffer from systematic domain shift
vulnerabilities that cannot be mitigated by post-hoc softmax calibration or synthetic lab
augmentation. Real-world deployment requires architectural decoupling via binary foliage gating
and targeted in-situ field data integration.

2. FORMAL PROBLEM STATEMENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Agricultural vision models deployed in production face three fundamental challenges:
1. The In-Domain vs. Field Generalization Gap: High test accuracy on lab-mounted leaves masks
   fragility when encountering natural variable lighting, complex soil backgrounds, and camera sensor noise.
2. Attractor Sink Collapse: Under severe distribution shift, overparameterized softmax heads map
   novel visual representations into high-prevalence majority classes rather than outputting low confidence.
3. Out-of-Distribution Vulnerability: Standard closed-set architectures lack an inherent rejection
   mechanism, generating false actionable disease diagnoses on non-crop leaves, soil, hands, and tools.

2B. FORMAL RESEARCH HYPOTHESES & EMPIRICAL VERDICTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
H1: Baseline Representational Capacity
    Hypothesis: MobileNetV2 with frozen ImageNet features is sufficient to achieve >80%
    classification accuracy across Maharashtra-relevant crops under lab conditions.
    Verdict: PARTIALLY SUPPORTED.
    Empirical Evidence: The >80% condition was achieved for 3 of 4 crops: Tomato (90.23%),
    Grape (89.04%), and Sugarcane (83.43%), but not for Chilli (63.79% on single-source COLD 2024
    lab data due to fine-grained symptomatic overlap).

H2: Domain Shift & Generalization Gap
    Hypothesis: Models trained primarily on laboratory-curated leaf images suffer a statistically
    significant performance collapse when evaluated on unconstrained real-world field photography.
    Verdict: SUPPORTED.
    Empirical Evidence: A 50 to 75 percentage point Generalization Gap was observed across all crops
    (Tomato: 90.23% -> 18.91%; Grape: 89.04% -> 11.43%; Chilli: 63.79% -> 50.60%; Sugarcane: 83.43% -> 35.00%),
    with predictions concentrating in majority attractor classes.

H3: Post-Hoc Confidence Calibration for OOD Gating
    Hypothesis: Post-hoc softmax temperature scaling on validation data will calibrate confidence
    estimates sufficiently to filter out-of-distribution non-leaf and wrong-crop inputs.
    Verdict: REFUTED.
    Empirical Evidence: While temperature scaling reduced in-domain ECE by >50%, 60%–73% of wrong-crop
    leaves and non-leaf distractors bypassed validation-selected confidence thresholds. Softmax confidence
    operates on closed-set relative logit margins and cannot detect semantic OOD shifts.

H4: Decoupled Stage 1 Foliage Gatekeeper
    Hypothesis: Decoupling foliage verification into a dedicated binary classifier (Leaf vs. Non-Leaf)
    trained on diverse non-leaf negatives creates an effective pre-filtering safeguard.
    Verdict: SUPPORTED.
    Empirical Evidence: The binary gatekeeper achieved 99.19% internal test accuracy, rejected 100.00%
    of Track D non-leaf distractors, and showed strong retention across the evaluated field-leaf benchmarks
    (96.55% field leaf retention across 2,229 uncurated field images).

H5: In-Situ Field-Data Retraining & Adaptation
    Hypothesis: Incorporating genuine in-situ field imagery into training data improves real-world
    field classification without degrading in-domain performance.
    Verdict: PARTIALLY SUPPORTED / MIXED EVIDENCE.
    Empirical Evidence: The field-improvement component was observed on evaluated benchmarks (Tomato
    Phase 18 accuracy increased from 18.91% to 20.22% with macro F1 +5.25 pp; Grape Phase 18 accuracy
    increased from 11.43% to 13.21% with macro F1 +2.00 pp; target class recalls surged: TYLCV recall from
    5.36% to 42.86%, Downy Mildew recall from 4.00% to 26.00%), but internal test accuracy declined modestly
    (Tomato: 90.23% -> 89.72%, -0.50 pp; Grape: 89.04% -> 87.86%, -1.18 pp). Therefore, the field-improvement
    component was observed, while the no-in-domain-degradation condition was not fully satisfied.

4. DISCUSSION & AGRONOMIC INTERPRETATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Why Lab-Trained Models Default to Attractor Sinks:
   In laboratory datasets, leaves are flattened against uniform backgrounds (black plastic, white paper)
   under controlled diffusers. In real fields, natural soil texture, shadows, and varying chlorophyll
   fluorescence present high visual complexity. Lacking training support on complex backgrounds, the
   network activates high-frequency edge filters that mimic the necrotic lesions of Late Blight or
   Early Blight.

2. Why Post-Hoc Softmax Calibration Fails for OOD Gating:
   Temperature scaling monotonically scales logits by a positive scalar, adjusting overconfident
   probabilities toward true accuracy on in-domain distributions. However, because out-of-domain
   inputs activate arbitrary unconstrained features in deep layers, the resulting softmax distribution
   often exhibits extreme peakiness. A scalar shift preserves logit ordering and fails to suppress
   incorrect peaks.

3. The Decoupled Gating Paradigm:
   Rather than expecting a fine-grained 33-class disease network to discern species identity and
   non-leaf distractors simultaneously, real-world systems must decouple the detection pipeline:
   - Stage 1: Dedicated binary foliage gatekeeper (Leaf vs Non-Leaf).
   - Stage 2: User-verified crop selection (avoiding brittle automated species classification under field domain shift).
   - Stage 3: Fine-grained disease diagnosis powered by mixed-domain training data.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. THREATS TO VALIDITY & LIMITATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Sample Size of Field Augmentation: Tomato received 71 field images; Grape received 176 images.
   While statistically meaningful deltas were observed on target classes, full-spectrum adaptation
   requires larger collections.
2. Incomplete Disease Representation: Several canonical diseases (Spider Mites, Target Spot, Bacterial
   Spot) currently possess near-zero authentic field representation in public archives.
3. Geographic Domain Shift: Acquired field imagery originated in Cameroon, Turkey, and Italy. Regional
   variations in soil color, cultivar canopy structure, and co-occurring weeds in Maharashtra may
   introduce secondary distribution shifts.
4. Grad-CAM Non-Causality: Saliency heatmaps visualize gradient magnitude, but do not provide proof
   of underlying causal reasoning or establish background shortcut causality.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. FINAL THESIS CONCLUSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This research proves that high in-domain classification accuracy on curated agricultural benchmarks
does not imply field readiness. Through an audited 2,556-evaluation robustness framework alongside
complete 4,741-sample internal test splits, we quantified a 50–75 percentage point Generalization Gap
and documented the failure modes of softmax calibration and laboratory species identification.

We demonstrated that authentic field data integration is uniquely effective: Chilli Candidate D
achieved a +20.48 pp field boost, and targeted Tomato/Grape field-data adaptation produced modest
field-benchmark gains while causing modest reductions in internal test performance, reducing the concentration
of predictions in previously dominant attractor classes in some evaluated cases and improving sensitivity to
selected field phenotypes. Finally, a robust binary Stage 1 Gatekeeper (99.19% test accuracy, 100% Track D
distractor rejection) showed strong retention across the evaluated field-leaf benchmarks (96.55% field leaf
retention across 2,229 uncurated field images), establishing a viable architectural safeguard for practical deployment.
